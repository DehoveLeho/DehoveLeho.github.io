Control Theory & Robotics

Can eight equations beat 80 years of PID control?

For the past two years in engineering school, I've had PID controllers drilled into my head in virtually every control systems class. I even traveled halfway around the world to Malaysia, studying alternative methods for model identification and parameter tuning. PID is everywhere and over 95% of industrial automation relies on it, but it made me wonder: why don't engineering curriculums show emerging machine learning techniques, like compact Multilayer Perceptrons, for state correction and complex nonlinear systems?

To investigate, I simulated a quadrotor carrying a suspended payload, a notoriously underactuated nonlinear system where moving the drone excites violent pendular swings. A natural question arises: couldn't a finely tuned PID do just as well? Around small angles (under 5 degrees), absolutely. Linear approximations hold, and a tuned PID provides near-optimal damping without any machine learning.

The problem appears during aggressive maneuvers or large disturbances. Above 20 degrees of tilt, nonlinear centrifugal and Coriolis couplings dominate. Fixed linear gains face an impossible tradeoff: tune aggressively to suppress payload oscillations, and the motors chatter and saturate; tune softly for stability, and the payload swings wildly past the target (27.3 cm average miss).

Control engineers traditionally tackle this with gain-scheduling lookup tables. Instead, a compact 2-layer MLP with 106 parameters (eight linear algebra equations in C) acts as a continuous nonlinear compensator. It automatically counter-pitches half a period before arrival to cancel angular momentum, cutting tracking error by 43% (15.5 cm average miss) with zero oscillatory ringing.

However, pure neural control has a fatal blind spot: sustained disturbances. By the Francis-Wonham Internal Model Principle, rejecting a constant external force (like a 1.4 N crosswind) mathematically requires an integrator. Because a feedforward MLP has no internal state, it suffers a permanent steady-state drift under wind.

Where does this actually matter in industry? For single-input linear loops (such as thermal heating elements, tank water levels, motor RPM, or certified avionics requiring strict mathematical stability proofs under DO-178C), a classical PID is strictly superior, bulletproof, and unbeatable in simplicity. Using a neural network there is unnecessary overkill.

The hybrid neural approach pays off on coupled, underactuated, or hard-to-model nonlinear systems: industrial overhead cranes dampening 40-ton container swings, aerial transport drones, soft robotic exoskeletons with rubber hysteresis, or autonomous vehicles recovering at the edge of tire grip limits.

The winning architecture is hybrid: a classical PID integral baseline guaranteeing zero steady-state error, augmented by a lightweight neural policy to eliminate nonlinear swing.

State Estimation & Bayesian Filtering

Can we predict the outcome of a coin toss in mid-air?

Lately, I’ve been diving into state estimation and the Orocos ecosystem in ROS. To challenge myself beyond standard 2D robot localization, I built a 3D coin-toss tracking simulator in C++ using OpenCV and Orocos BFL (Bayesian Filtering Library).

A coin toss isn't random magic, but pure deterministic physics. If a coin is launched with a vertical velocity of 4.5 m/s and a spin rate of 6 Hz, its trajectory is mathematically pre-determined. If you can estimate the state vector in the first milliseconds of flight, you can calculate the landing face before impact.

I set up a 4D state vector X = [z; dz; theta; omega]. Under a simulated 30 Hz camera with Gaussian measurement noise (R = 0.25 px²), I benchmarked two BFL estimators: an EKF (with hand-calculated Jacobians) and an SMC Particle Filter (with about 600 particles).

The EKF was incredibly fast, executing in just 0.04 ms per step. It locked onto the 37.7 rad/s spin in 5 frames (~166 ms of flight), dropping estimation error to <1.2% while the tracking covariance fell from 10.0 to 0.04. The Particle Filter took 4.2 ms per step; though slower, it handled temporary face occlusions perfectly by tracking multi-modal probability distributions before collapsing them.

Testing the camera's observability limits revealed a fascinating control theory trap. At a 45° viewing angle, the EKF prediction accuracy reached 96.4% after 200 ms. Whereas at a 90° angle (looking directly at the coin's thin edge), the Jacobian became ill-conditioned. The covariance exploded from 0.01 to 4.2, and prediction accuracy collapsed to 51.5% which is essentially a random guess.

I also loved BFL's "honest" confidence. Flat landings yielded 98% confidence, but edge landings (vertical impact) dropped the EKF's confidence to 54%. The filter literally knows when it doesn't know.

In the real world, a 30 FPS webcam would just capture a gray motion blur. You'd need a nice industrial camera or event-based sensors. Plus, timing is brutal because at 5 Hz, a 15 ms catch-height timing error shifts the predicted angle by 26°, flipping a predicted "Heads" to a physical "Tails". But in a controlled setup (like conveyor belt sorting), it's totally viable.

This was a great way get hands-on experience with BFL before I move onto Orocos KDL and iTaSC.
